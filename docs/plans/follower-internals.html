What the follower is actually doing

The follower solves two separate control problems twenty times a second: where to point the wheels, and how hard to drive them. They share a path and almost nothing else. This is a working map of both — the real parameters, the real measured constants, and where the lap time is currently going on the a7.0 line.

01 — Orientation

One loop, two independent problems

At control_hz: 20 the follower runs the same sequence every tick. The steering half and the throttle half both read the path, but they never read each other. A steering change cannot fix a speed deficit, and no amount of throttle tuning will stop the car running wide.

1

Locate the car on the path

Find the nearest path point to the current pose. Everything downstream is indexed off this point — the curvature under the car, the planned speed, the planned acceleration.

2

Estimate own speed

A tire-model observer driven by wheel encoders produces v_est. This is not a measurement — it is a simulation of the tire, corrected by pose. Section 06.

3

Pick a lookahead point → steering

Walk forward along the path until you are Ld metres away. Fit a circular arc to that point and command its curvature. Sections 02–03.

4

Sample the planned speed → v_target

Read the raceline's own v_mps column at a point ahead of the car, because the throttle you set now will not land for ~0.15 s. Section 04.

5

Convert v_target into a wheel-speed command

Throttle in this simulator is a wheel-speed command, not a torque. Choose a slip, bounded by the tire's peak-force band. Section 05.

The important asymmetry

The raceline file carries its own velocity profile (v_mps) that already respects the acceleration and grip budget it was solved with. The follower's job is not to decide how fast to go. It is to land the car on the profile's speed at the profile's position. Every longitudinal parameter exists to close the gap between "the plan says 7.2 m/s here" and "the wheel actually delivered it 0.15 s ago".

02 — Lateral control

Pure pursuit is one arc, chosen every tick

The whole algorithm: pick a point on the path Ld metres ahead, then steer along the unique circular arc that starts at the rear axle, points where the car points, and passes through that point. Next tick, throw it away and pick a new one.

If that point sits y metres to the side in the car's own frame, the arc's curvature is:

κ = 2y / Ld² → δ = atan(κ · L) // bicycle model, L = wheelbase 0.324 m

That is the entire steering law. Everything else in the lateral half of the config exists to choose Ld well, because Ld alone decides whether the car cuts the apex, runs wide, or weaves.

Why steer_a_lat_max = 7.0 is load-bearing

The front tire's lateral force peaks at about 1.0 g near 0.57° of slip and has fallen to 0.5 g by 5.7°. Past the peak, a geometric controller's instinct — more steering — is the wrong sign: slip angle grows, force drops, the car runs wider still. Measured at the 6.5 rung, that spiral took the car from 0.2 m to 0.9 m wide in one second at full lock.

Capping commanded curvature at a_cap / v² holds the steering where the force is still near maximum and lets the car run wide by the honest physical amount instead of spiralling. Raising this cap to 8.5 was tried: 146 collisions, no completed laps.

03 — Lateral tuning

Ld is not one number, it is a chain

The base rule is speed-proportional — fast means look further — then three corrections apply in order. Each was added to fix a specific measured failure, and each has an off switch.

Ld = clamp(lookahead_min, lookahead_k · v, lookahead_max) ÷ (1 + lookahead_curv_gain · max|κ| within Ld) // tighten in corners → capped so the chord's sagitta ≤ lookahead_sag_frac · inside margin → k and max scaled by (cmd_delay / lookahead_delay_ref) // slow loop, shorter Ld

Reading the symptom

Weaving on fast straights (lateral error changing sign > 1.2 ×/s) means Ld is too short — lengthen lookahead_max. Cutting the apex (signed offset toward the inside, positive corner bias) means it is too long in corners — raise lookahead_curv_gain or lower lookahead_k. Running wide is neither: that is the tire, and the answer is a lower lateral rung on the line, not a lookahead change.

Where this bites you right now

On the 20-lap baseline, every fast lap (11.75–11.80 s) held max lateral error ≤ 0.10 m, and every slow lap had an excursion to 0.23–0.60 m. The spread from 11.75 best to 11.92 median is lateral, not longitudinal — the speed controller is not giving up on those laps, the car is running wide and paying for it.

04 — Longitudinal control

Where v_target comes from — and why it is early

The raceline CSV carries a v_mps column: the speed profile the optimiser solved, already containing its own braking distances. The follower does not recompute it. It samples it — at a point ahead of the car:

lead = v · (cmd_delay + target_lead_s) + lead_min_m v_target = clamp(path_v[ near + lead ], v_min, v_max)

The cmd_delay part of that lead is correct and necessary: a throttle published now does not reach the wheel for ~0.15 s, by which time the car has moved. Sampling the profile where the command will land is exactly right.

target_lead_s is extra preview on top of that — and it is the one number in this chain with no physical justification on this track.

This is the live finding

Drag target_lead_s between 0 and 0.08 and watch the blue trace in the braking zones. At 8 m/s the shipped 0.08 pushes the sample point roughly 2.1 m down the road — and this track's braking zones are about 7.8 m long. The car starts braking about a quarter of the zone early, at every corner, on every lap.

That value was tuned for Porto, whose braking zones are short. The multi-track branch measured the same effect on this track independently and priced it at 0.28 s per lap, which is why their registry sets it to 0.0. Your yaml still has 0.08.

Why the profile is sampled, not previewed

An older version took the minimum of the profile over a fixed 1 m preview. That reaches corner speed a metre early at every entry and holds it there: measured at ~0.11 s per corner, about 0.55 s a lap. The profile already contains its own braking distances — taking a minimum over a window brakes for a corner twice.

05 — Longitudinal control

Throttle is a wheel speed, so tuning means choosing a slip

This is the single most important thing about the simulator's longitudinal model. The sim spins the wheel to u = 25.25 × throttle regardless of the car. Throttle is not torque. It is a wheel-speed command.

Force therefore comes from the difference between wheel surface speed and car speed — the slip ratio — through the tire's friction curve:

S = (u − v) / max(v, v_slip_den) // PhysX longitudinal slip, v_slip_den = 4 m/s a = μ(S) · g − DRAG_LIN · v // DRAG_LIN = 0.273 /s

μ(S) is not a straight line. It rises to a peak of 0.72 at S = 0.15, then falls to an asymptote of 0.464 at S = 0.25. Commanding more slip past the peak gives you less force — the longitudinal twin of the steering cap in section 02.

The friction circle option

The multi-track branch added slip_circle: instead of a fixed band, use one value at zero lateral load and scale it down as the corner loads the tire.

band = max( slip_circle · √(1 − (a_lat / steer_a_lat_max)²) , 0.02 )

The shape is right — a tire carrying 6 m/s² sideways genuinely has less left for driving. Set the lateral slider above and watch the band close.

Two things to know before you enable it

It replaces slip_accel entirely. With slip_circle > 0, your measured 0.16 does nothing — the startup log prints the fixed band and then says it has been superseded. On your host the circle's zero-load value of 0.12 sits below your measured 0.16 optimum.

It has not worked on this machine. The multi-track config that enables it — circle band + acceleration feedforward + longer lookahead, on their hardened line — gave 11 collisions in 26 laps here, against your 1-in-20 with the circle off. That config was validated on the Linux box.

Where the band sits around

The band is not centred on the car's current speed — that would be gone by the time the command lands. It is centred on v_land, the speed the car is predicted to have cmd_delay seconds from now, using the profile's planned acceleration rather than the observer's. That choice matters: predicting with the observer's own acceleration closes a feedback loop of delay 0.15 s and gain ~5, which oscillated at 1 Hz and stuck the car at walking pace.

06 — State estimation

The controller has to guess its own speed

Every longitudinal decision above needs v, and the car does not have a speedometer. speed_source: tire runs a small forward simulation: take the wheel speed from the encoders, run it through the same friction curve the simulator uses, integrate. The result is bounded-error by construction — the tire model cannot produce a wildly wrong speed the way a raw integration can.

Two failure modes are already documented in the code, and both cost real time:

The encoder aliasing bug — your fix

The bridge stamps messages with its own publish time, not the moment the simulator advanced the wheel. When it publishes faster than the sim steps, it stalls and then dumps the backlog. Measured here: one message with a 0.118 s gap and 0.350 rad of travel, the next with 0.007 s and 5.881 rad — reading 51.9 m/s against a 22.88 m/s vehicle limit.

Differentiating over a fixed 0.05 s window instead of message-to-message fixes the arithmetic at any publish rate. Replayed on the raw capture: p95 slip 4.65 → 3.21, max 51.91 → 8.96, impossible reads 1.12% → 0.00%. Worth 3.7 s per lap on this host. On a machine where the bridge publishes at the sim's rate the window spans one message and the change is a no-op by construction.

The one-wheel optimism

The observer simulates a single wheel at the car's speed. Real contact patches run at different speeds — inner/outer differ by yaw rate × track/2, fronts by 1/cos(steer). The friction curve is concave, so four wheels at spread slips deliver less force than one at the mean, and the one-wheel model reads high exactly where the spread is large: +0.15–0.19 m/s at hairpin apexes.

observer_wheels: 4 fixes the bias and stays off anyway. With the true speed in hand the follower asked for more out of the left-leg apex at full lock, the fronts lost lateral grip, and the car understeered into the wall. The optimism was doubling as a throttle limiter.

On the 20-lap baseline v_est − v_true was negative on every single lap, between −0.06 and −0.27 m/s. The estimator reads consistently low, and the slip band is centred on it — worth keeping in view as you tune.

08 — Method

How a change gets accepted here

Most of the numbers above were not chosen — they were measured, and several of the obvious-looking improvements were tried and reverted. The protocol matters as much as the parameter.

1

Reset the environment first

lap_count and collision_count are cumulative since the simulator started. A run launched on top of a previous one inherits its totals and cannot be compared to anything.

2

Confirm the car is actually stopped

The sim latches the last throttle it received. A killed follower leaves the car driving with Publisher count: 0 on both command topics — it looks stopped and is not. Check odom linear.x reads 0.0.

3

Change exactly one variable

Pass it as a launch override so the baseline file is untouched and the startup log records what actually ran.

4

Twenty laps, no more

The sim tick is stable at 17.7–19.4 Hz over 20 laps but sags to ~14 Hz in a long session, and lap times move with it. Keep runs short and restart between them.

5

Judge against the plan, not the previous run

The a7.0 line plans 11.441 s. That number does not move, so it is the only stable yardstick. Zero collisions is a gate, not a trade.
